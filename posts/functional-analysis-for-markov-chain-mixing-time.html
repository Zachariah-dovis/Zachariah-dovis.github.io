Functional Analysis for Markov Chain Mixing Time

Zachariah Zhang Jul 18, 2026 5 min read

This post is a brief summary of Kuikui Liu's 6.S891 Lecture 12, \(\textit{Functional Analytic Tools for MCMC}\). The lecture explains how functional inequalities give a unified way to prove rapid mixing for reversible Markov chains, especially local chains such as Glauber dynamics.

1. The functional-analytic viewpoint#

Let \(\mu\) be a probability distribution on a finite state space \(\Omega\), and let \(P\) be a Markov chain reversible with respect to \(\mu\). The goal is to control the total variation mixing time \(T_{\mathrm{mix}}(\varepsilon)\). The lecture starts from a useful general principle: instead of trying to analyze total variation distance directly, choose a smoother divergence \(D(\cdot\Vert\cdot)\) and prove that it contracts under one step of the chain.

\(D(\nu P\Vert \mu) \le (1-\alpha)D(\nu\Vert\mu)\) for all initial laws \(\nu\)
\(\Longrightarrow\) rapid mixing after roughly \(\alpha^{-1}\log D(\delta_x\Vert\mu)\) steps.

The quantity \(D\) does not need to be a metric. What matters is that small \(D(\nu\Vert\mu)\) implies small total variation distance.

Two standard examples fit into this template. In path coupling, one takes \(D\) to be the Wasserstein distance \(W_1\) on a graph structure over \(\Omega\). It is enough to check contraction only for neighboring states, which is the Bubley--Dyer simplification. In the spectral-gap method, one takes

\[ D(\nu\Vert\mu)=\chi^2(\nu\Vert\mu)=\operatorname{Var}_\mu\!\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}\right), \]

and the relevant functional inequality is the Poincare inequality

\[ \gamma\operatorname{Var}_\mu(f)\le \mathcal E_P(f,f), \qquad \mathcal E_P(f,f)=\frac12\sum_{x,y}\mu(x)P(x,y)(f(x)-f(y))^2. \]

Here \(\gamma\) is the spectral gap. This method is powerful, but for many Gibbs distributions it loses an extra factor because \(\chi^2(\delta_x\Vert\mu)=1/\mu(x)-1\) can be exponentially large.

2. Entropy and log-Sobolev inequalities#

The lecture then turns to relative entropy, or KL-divergence, because its worst-case initial size is only logarithmic in \(1/\mu_{\min}\):

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\nu\Vert\mu)=\sum_{x\in\Omega}\nu(x)\log\frac{\nu(x)}{\mu(x)}, \qquad \max_x D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\delta_x\Vert\mu)=\log\frac1{\mu_{\min}}. \]

For a nonnegative function \(f\), write

\[ \operatorname{Ent}_\mu(f)=\mathbb E_\mu[f\log f]-\mathbb E_\mu[f]\log\mathbb E_\mu[f]. \]

Then \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\nu\Vert\mu)=\operatorname{Ent}_\mu(d\nu/d\mu)\). The decay of this entropy is controlled by log-Sobolev inequalities.

Definition

A reversible Markov chain satisfies a standard log-Sobolev inequality with constant \(\kappa\) if

\[ \kappa\operatorname{Ent}_\mu(f)\le \mathcal E_P(\sqrt f,\sqrt f). \]

It satisfies a modified log-Sobolev inequality with constant \(\rho\) if

\[ \rho\operatorname{Ent}_\mu(f)\le \mathcal E_P(f,\log f). \]

The modified version is often better suited for mixing-time applications because the standard constant \(\kappa\) can be sensitive to tiny atom masses of \(\mu\).

3. Main consequences#

Lower bounds on \(\rho(P)\) or \(\kappa(P)\) imply mixing bounds with only a \(\log\log(1/\mu_{\min})\) dependence on the smallest stationary mass:

\[ T_{\mathrm{mix}}(\varepsilon) \le \frac1{\rho(P)}\left(\log\log\frac1{\mu_{\min}}+\log\frac1{2\varepsilon^2}\right), \]

and similarly with \(4\kappa(P)\) in place of \(\rho(P)\). This is the main reason entropy methods can give nearly-linear mixing where spectral-gap methods may only give a suboptimal bound.

Concentration

The same constants also imply sub-Gaussian concentration. If

\[ v(f)=\max_x\sum_y P(x,y)(f(x)-f(y))^2, \]

then for \(t\ge0\),

\[ \Pr_\mu[f\ge \mathbb E_\mu f+t] \le \exp\left(-\frac{\rho(P)t^2}{2v(f)}\right). \]

Thus log-Sobolev inequalities are simultaneously mixing tools and concentration tools.

The constants satisfy the comparison

\[ 4\kappa(P)\le \rho(P)\le 2\gamma(P). \]

So proving a modified log-Sobolev inequality is generally stronger than proving a spectral gap, but in many spin-system regimes where efficient algorithms exist these constants are expected to be of comparable order.

4. Heat semigroup proof idea#

A key technical device is to pass from discrete time to continuous time. Let the chain make transitions at the arrival times of independent exponential clocks. The number of jumps by time \(t\) is Poisson with mean \(t\), so the resulting transition operator is

\[ H_t=\sum_{k\ge0} e^{-t}\frac{t^k}{k!}P^k =\exp[-t(I-P)]. \]

This is the heat semigroup of \(P\). If \(f_t=d(\nu H_t)/d\mu\), then entropy evolves according to

\[ \frac d{dt}\operatorname{Ent}_\mu(f_t) = -\mathcal E_P(f_t,\log f_t). \]

The modified log-Sobolev inequality gives

\[ \frac d{dt}\operatorname{Ent}_\mu(f_t) \le -\rho(P)\operatorname{Ent}_\mu(f_t), \]

and Gronwall's inequality yields exponential entropy decay:

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\nu H_t\Vert\mu) \le e^{-\rho(P)t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\nu\Vert\mu). \]

This differential calculation is the cleanest explanation for why the Dirichlet form \(\mathcal E_P(f,\log f)\) appears in the modified log-Sobolev inequality.

5. The Herbst argument#

The lecture also sketches how modified log-Sobolev inequalities imply concentration. The proof uses the moment generating function and shows

\[ \mathbb E_\mu[e^{sf}] \le \exp\left(s\mathbb E_\mu f+s^2\frac{v(f)}{2\rho(P)}\right). \]

Combining this bound with Markov's inequality and optimizing over \(s\) gives the sub-Gaussian tail stated above. Conceptually, the same entropy dissipation that drives mixing also prevents Lipschitz observables from having heavy tails under the stationary measure.

6. Takeaway#

The main message of the lecture is that functional analysis provides a common language for mixing proofs. Coupling proves contraction in transportation distance; Poincare inequalities prove contraction in \(\chi^2\); modified log-Sobolev inequalities prove contraction in entropy. For modern sampling problems, especially Gibbs distributions and spin systems, entropy-based tools are particularly valuable because they can remove the extra losses caused by the exponentially small value of \(\mu_{\min}\).

In the broader story of approximate sampling, this lecture prepares the ground for later results showing that correlation decay, zero-free regions, and spectral independence can imply strong functional inequalities, and therefore nearly-linear mixing of local Markov chains.
